# Evidence and Testimony

The cards use synthetic examples. Record design intent and observed results separately.

<a id="red-herring"></a>

## Red herring

Playbook position `26.3`. Technique.

- **Selection.** An investigation needs a plausible wrong inference that remains fair to revise.
- **Game application.** Offer an authentic signal with several explanations. Track fact, available evidence and the inference selected by a player separately.
- **Intended effect.** A player can pursue a reasonable but incorrect lead and revise the lead after obtaining a corrective source.
- **Misuse risk.** A clue hidden from all routes or a solution that contradicts established facts turns misdirection into arbitrary withholding.
- **Example.** Fresh mud beside the pump suggests a dock worker entered at night. A drainage diagram and an opened floodgate show that the water deposited the same mud during an earlier surge.
- **Evidence.** Trace the genuine mud observation, incorrect suspect choice and independently available corrective diagram. Ask a player what justified reconsidering the suspect.

<a id="rashomon"></a>

## Rashomon effect

Playbook position `26.4`. Technique.

- **Selection.** Accounts of one event matter because witnesses had different access, values or motives.
- **Game application.** Fix the shared event where canon settles the event. Record attributed versions with vantage and evidence. Keep unresolved author decisions explicit.
- **Intended effect.** A player can compare testimony and decide what confidence each account deserves.
- **Misuse risk.** Contradictory speech alone establishes neither multiple truths nor an unresolved world event.
- **Example.** A guard reports an intentional floodgate opening. A deckhand saw a rope break. The journal fixes the gate time while leaving the actor's motive open.
- **Evidence.** Inspect the evidence available to each speaker and verify that the journal can constrain event time without settling motive.

<a id="liar-paradox"></a>

## Liar paradox

Playbook position `26.8`. Analytical problem. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Liar Paradox](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/liar-paradox/index.html)

- **Selection.** A puzzle concerns truth evaluation and a claim with semantic self-reference.
- **Game application.** Separate a semantic self-reference from deceit, mistaken observation and conflicting testimony. Write rules for the actual logic task before connecting a puzzle to a quest gate.
- **Intended effect.** A player can recognize why a simple truth assignment fails in the constructed puzzle.
- **Misuse risk.** Ordinary lies need motive or evidence, not a semantic paradox explanation.
- **Example.** An archive seal asserts that the seal's current assertion is false. Compare a dock worker who falsely claims to have closed a gate earlier.
- **Evidence.** Try both truth assignments to the archive seal. Verify the dock worker claim can be evaluated against independent gate evidence without contradiction from self-reference.
