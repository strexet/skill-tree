# Information and Turns

The cards use synthetic examples. Record design intent and observed results separately.

<a id="setup-payoff"></a>

## Setup and payoff

Playbook position `27.1`. Technique.

- **Selection.** A later action is reachable but has no legible reason, or an early promise has no consequence.
- **Game application.** Give the player a recognizable fact, technique or object before a later beat depends on the preparation. Link the intended payoff and identify an alternate preparation when the first source can be skipped.
- **Intended effect.** The player sees how an earlier encounter makes the later choice or consequence intelligible.
- **Misuse risk.** An optional setup cannot be the sole basis of required comprehension. A reachability pass can miss a weak payoff.
- **Example.** A practice lock shows that the worn handle only releases after a pause. The later floodgate uses the same pattern. A warning can teach the pattern on the route that skips practice.
- **Evidence.** Trace the main and skip routes to the same decision. Ask a participant how the player character could know when to release the handle.

<a id="foreshadowing"></a>

## Foreshadowing

Playbook position `27.2`. Technique.

- **Selection.** A causal turn is already possible but feels arbitrary in hindsight.
- **Game application.** Plant a weak early signal with a readable interpretation after disclosure. Preserve the difference between a retrospective hint and mandatory preparation.
- **Intended effect.** After the reveal, a player can recognize a prior detail as meaningful.
- **Misuse risk.** A conspicuous cue can expose the twist too early. A hint does not repair a missing causal prerequisite.
- **Example.** Several rescue logs omit tide height. Later the player learns that the meter was disabled. The omission suggests deliberate concealment after the reveal.
- **Evidence.** Compare recognition after the reveal with predictions before the reveal. Verify the relevant clue remains available where comprehension requires a clue.

<a id="dramatic-irony"></a>

## Dramatic irony

Playbook position `27.3`. Technique.

- **Selection.** Tension can arise from a consequential difference between player and participant knowledge.
- **Game application.** Name what each participant knows and when each disclosure occurred. Give the player a response appropriate to the difference.
- **Intended effect.** A player can anticipate a consequence while a participant acts from a different belief.
- **Misuse risk.** Player exposure cannot silently supply NPC knowledge, and a route that omits the clue may need a different scene response.
- **Example.** A player saw a counterfeit key enter the seal box. The foreman confidently prepares to use the key. The player can warn the foreman or wait for a demonstration.
- **Evidence.** Trace seen and skipped disclosure routes. Check available lines against each participant's evidence.

<a id="reversal"></a>

## Causal reversal

Playbook position `27.4`. Technique.

- **Selection.** A scene moves directly toward the expected result and an existing cause can change the position.
- **Game application.** Use an established action or obligation to produce a credible contrary consequence. Track the cause and changed stakes rather than relying on a surprise label.
- **Intended effect.** A player can recognize why an attempted solution creates a new problem.
- **Misuse risk.** A surprise that requires a new world rule or unavailable information is an unsupported turn.
- **Example.** A forged exemption delivered to clear a ferryman instead exposes a protected fraud register. A stamped duplicate at the office establishes how the evidence links both cases.
- **Evidence.** Trace the exemption and duplicate stamp before the hearing. On a skip route, provide another stamp source or mark the turning point as unsupported.

<a id="recognition"></a>

## Recognition and reinterpretation

Playbook position `27.5`. Technique.

- **Selection.** New information can make an earlier action mean something different without changing event history.
- **Game application.** Reveal a relationship, intention or cause that reinterprets an established fact. Keep previously established event, access and source evidence stable.
- **Intended effect.** A player can describe a new interpretation of a familiar act.
- **Misuse risk.** A reveal that contradicts earlier facts is a retcon candidate. A new explanation needs compatible evidence.
- **Example.** A pilot's daily detour looked like smuggling. A signed agreement reveals that the pilot keeps a promise to deliver medicine to an isolated berth.
- **Evidence.** Compare the timeline before and after disclosure. Ask which facts stayed the same and what the agreement changed about the interpretation.

<a id="anticipation-danger"></a>

## Anticipation of danger

Playbook position `27.6`. Technique.

- **Selection.** A threat needs tension before the event and the player can act on the warning.
- **Game application.** Reveal a threat early enough for preparation, route choice or informed risk. Tie escalating cues to player response.
- **Intended effect.** A player can make a deliberate tradeoff in advance of a dangerous event.
- **Misuse risk.** A countdown that cannot change any decision can serve atmosphere but does not prove meaningful preparation.
- **Example.** A lighthouse beam stutters before a night crossing. The player can take a spare lamp at the cost of cargo space or depart quickly.
- **Evidence.** Verify the warning arrives before the decision and each preparation action changes the risk or available resources.
