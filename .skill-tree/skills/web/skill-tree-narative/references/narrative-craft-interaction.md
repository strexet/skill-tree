# Character Interaction Patterns

The cards use synthetic examples. Record design intent and observed results separately.

<a id="tactic-change"></a>

## Goal, obstacle and tactic change

Playbook position `27.7`. Technique.

- **Selection.** An exchange delivers information but the speaker's action never encounters resistance.
- **Game application.** Name a goal and obstacle, then change tactic after the obstacle has a visible effect. Track a request, bargain or disclosure as an attempt.
- **Intended effect.** A player can understand why a participant shifts from one kind of action to another.
- **Misuse risk.** A quiet orientation scene can serve a purpose without conflict. A tactic change without pressure may read as arbitrary.
- **Example.** A mechanic asks for a ferry ticket, meets a refusal and then offers to repair the manifest printer. The bargain pursues the same transport goal.
- **Evidence.** Compare the exposition-only version and the bargaining version. Verify the refusal changes the available approach and the original motivation survives.

<a id="foil-character"></a>

## Foil character

Playbook position `27.8`. Technique.

- **Selection.** A character's stated value remains abstract and a comparable decision can expose a real difference.
- **Game application.** Give two characters an overlapping concern and distinct commitments under similar pressure. Show how the commitments produce different justified actions.
- **Intended effect.** A player can infer the characters' values by comparing behavior.
- **Misuse risk.** A foil should not become a list of reversed traits or an unmotivated opponent.
- **Example.** Both wardens protect vulnerable residents. One releases evacuation data immediately to reduce danger, while the other first removes household names to protect privacy.
- **Evidence.** Inspect the justifications, costs and actions of both wardens. Check that a shared value survives the contrast.

<a id="status-shift"></a>

## Conversational status shift

Playbook position `27.9`. Technique.

- **Selection.** Power between speakers remains static although new information or resources could change who decides.
- **Game application.** Show an actual source of leverage, such as control of an exit or new evidence, and let speech or action reflect changed decision authority.
- **Intended effect.** A player notices the person who now determines the next action.
- **Misuse risk.** A stronger tone without changed leverage can be a change in tactic rather than status.
- **Example.** A foreman orders a deckhand to launch. The deckhand obtains the only certified lock key and makes the foreman request permission.
- **Evidence.** Compare the available actions before and after the key changes hands. Check that the new option belongs to the person with leverage.

<a id="action-reaction-decision"></a>

## Action, reaction and decision

Playbook position `27.10`. Technique.

- **Selection.** A consequential event is followed immediately by another instruction and leaves no visible aftermath.
- **Game application.** Provide a response beat and a subsequent choice that reflects the event. A quiet reaction can carry the necessary change.
- **Intended effect.** A player can connect the loss to the next decision or understand the change in emotional context.
- **Misuse risk.** A mandatory extra twist can crowd out useful stillness and erase the cost of an event.
- **Example.** After a ferry loses the final crate, the player sees the crew clear the empty deck and can choose between salvaging rope or informing the family waiting ashore.
- **Evidence.** Check that the reaction follows the loss and the next decision reflects a real consequence. Ask whether the pause makes the consequence clear.

<a id="self-description-behavior"></a>

## Self-description and behavior

Playbook position `27.11`. Technique.

- **Selection.** A character is conveyed by a label and behavior never gives the label practical meaning.
- **Game application.** Compare what the character claims with one observable choice. Consider deliberate masking, a blind spot or a competing value.
- **Intended effect.** A player can infer a tension in the character without requiring a speech explaining the tension.
- **Misuse risk.** A contradiction alone is not a continuity defect when motive and situation support the contrast.
- **Example.** A clerk describes every favor as a transaction. The clerk opens a shelter before learning whether the residents can repay the favor.
- **Evidence.** Trace both the stated rule and the shelter action. Check whether the scene supports an unspoken commitment or needs more evidence.
