# Expression and Player Effects

The cards use synthetic examples. Record design intent and observed results separately.

<a id="hemingway-iceberg"></a>

## Hemingway's iceberg

Playbook position `26.5`. Technique. [Hemingway interview](https://www.theparisreview.org/interviews/4825/the-art-of-fiction-no-21-ernest-hemingway)

- **Selection.** Direct explanation repeats information already supported by actions, objects or speech.
- **Game application.** Choose an omitted motive grounded in the source and carry a legible signal into play. Hemingway distinguishes deliberate omission from gaps in author knowledge.
- **Intended effect.** A player can infer grief or obligation from the participant's behavior.
- **Misuse risk.** Omitting the only motivation cue can remove the cause from the scene rather than create subtext.
- **Example.** A captain refuses to move a torn berth sign and repairs a second life vest before every sailing. The player sees the crew roster with one missing name.
- **Evidence.** Inspect the behavior and roster routes. Ask what the captain appears to be protecting. A route with no evidence keeps the motive uncertain.

<a id="defamiliarization"></a>

## Shklovsky's defamiliarization

Playbook position `26.6`. Technique. [Shklovsky, Art as Technique](https://www.opojaz.ru/manifests/kakpriem.html)

- **Selection.** A repeated activity has become automatic and conceals a meaningful social or material feature.
- **Game application.** Change the player's relationship to a familiar action so attention returns to the action. Shklovsky's account informs a game-specific adaptation.
- **Intended effect.** A player notices who performs hidden work and how the harbor depends on that work.
- **Misuse risk.** An unusual visual effect alone may obscure the action without changing perception.
- **Example.** Instead of collecting water from an instant tap, the player carries a leaking bucket through stairs used by maintenance crews and sees who usually repairs the leaks.
- **Evidence.** Compare what a participant notices on the normal and manual water routes. Check that the alternative still communicates the goal.

<a id="karpman-triangle"></a>

## Karpman's triangle

Playbook position `26.7`. Analytical model. [Karpman, Fairy Tales and Script Drama Analysis](https://karpmandramatriangle.com/pdf/DramaTriangle.pdf)

- **Selection.** A conflict may benefit from noticing who pressures, rescues or experiences loss of control in a particular beat.
- **Game application.** Use the role diagram to explore possible shifts between pursuer, rescuer and victim roles in a scene. Treat role labels as temporary interpretive hypotheses tied to behavior.
- **Intended effect.** A player can follow a change in who helps, pressures or depends on whom.
- **Misuse risk.** A role diagram cannot diagnose a person or establish a permanent character type. Cooperative negotiation may require a different lens.
- **Example.** A foreman criticizes a frightened deckhand. A bystander intervenes, then the deckhand uses spare parts as leverage and the foreman asks for help. A later routine parts exchange has no rescue or pressure dynamic.
- **Evidence.** Mark the actions that motivate each role assignment. Leave the cooperative exchange unclassified if role pressure is absent.

## Narrative transportation

Playbook position `26.9`. Perceived effect. [Green and Brock, narrative transportation study](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/the-role-of-transportation-in-the-persuasiveness-of-public-narrat/)

- **Selection.** A work aims for sustained attention, imagery and emotional involvement and needs evidence of the experience.
- **Game application.** Treat transportation as a candidate effect of a complete experience. Green and Brock study absorption in narratives. Record the design goal and suitable observations without borrowing a numeric scale or neurological explanation for a game.
- **Intended effect.** A participant stays oriented in the scene and describes mentally imagining relevant events or caring about the outcome.
- **Misuse risk.** Runtime completion, decorative detail and a fast input rate cannot establish absorption by themselves.
- **Example.** A ferry evacuation removes repeated status text after introducing the danger through route sounds and character action. The hypothesis is that fewer interruptions support attention to crew and tide.
- **Evidence.** Ask open questions about memorable moments and attention breaks. Compare responses and observed interruptions across equivalent sessions before making a player-effect claim.
