# Composition Models

The cards use synthetic examples. Record design intent and observed results separately.

<a id="freytag"></a>

## Freytag's pyramid

Playbook position `26.1`. Analytical model. [Freytag, The Technique of the Drama](https://www.briantriber.com/WritingSamples/Freytag_Drama/Freytag_11_Ch2_P2.html)

- **Selection.** A bounded dramatic line needs a review of escalation, counteraction and consequences.
- **Game application.** Freytag divides a complete dramatic action into introduction, rise, climax, return or fall, and catastrophe. The motivating event, onset of counteraction and final suspense connect the parts. Freytag treats the last two connecting crises as optional. A game adaptation maps the parts onto a selected playable path and checks what actions can change.
- **Intended effect.** A player should understand the escalation and the consequences after the turning point.
- **Misuse risk.** A short quiet scene can serve a local function. The model does not require every open route to share a curve, or the falling action to remove suspense.
- **Example.** A ferry crew introduces a failing lock, the player collects repair parts, a rushed repair traps the ferry, the rising tide pressures evacuation, and the remaining crew reach shore or lose cargo according to player action.
- **Evidence.** Map the five parts and connecting moments to the evacuation route. Check the ferry loss or recovery against the chosen actions and inspect the post-turn stakes.

## Harmon's story circle

Playbook position `26.2`. Analytical model. Author explanation cited in playbook Section 26.

- **Selection.** A playable arc needs a clearer relation between desire, action, cost and return.
- **Game application.** Harmon's sequence starts in a familiar situation, establishes desire, enters unfamiliar circumstances, adapts, gains the desired object, pays a cost, returns and changes. An interactive adaptation traces a path and records the actual decision and terminal state, which may show mixed change or continued commitment.
- **Intended effect.** A player should recognize a cost that follows an action and understand how the return reflects the selected path.
- **Misuse risk.** A diagram cannot authorize a character transformation or assert that a player chose a rejected action. A local pause can work without the full sequence.
- **Example.** A harbor apprentice leaves a familiar post to secure a signal lamp, learns smuggler codes, obtains the lamp and spends a favor. The return can renew the apprentice's commitment to public safety or weaken trust according to the bargain.
- **Evidence.** Compare two available bargains, actual costs and the return beats. Inspect a quiet lantern scene independently of the full arc.
