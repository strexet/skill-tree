# Quest Design

Use a quest card to specify what players can attempt and how the story responds. A short sketch is sufficient for a straightforward encounter. Add route and outcome detail when optional content, multiple endings or state persistence can change play.

## Intake and card

1. Identify the work, continuity, arc or current sequence, intended player experience and function of the quest. Record pending creative decisions with the exact scope and owner.
2. Define entry trigger, eligibility, time window, actors, resources and playable alternatives. Mark main plot, side content or an optional stage separately from technical access.
3. Name stages with stable scene or beat IDs. For each decisive stage record the player verb, available choices, required knowledge and outcome IDs. Give each exit a state effect, visible consequence and next entry point.
4. Distinguish authored intent, current implementation, planned change and observed evidence. Use the route contract for mechanical checks. Keep exact executable dialogue in Ink when an implementation exists.

| Card field | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Identity and purpose | Which work and arc use the quest, and what should the player experience? |
| Entry and window | What triggers the quest, who must be present and when can players enter? |
| Stages and choices | What can players attempt at each stable beat, and what limits each action? |
| Outcomes and exits | What changes, how is the change visible and where can the player continue? |
| Optional content | What value can the player miss, and which required clues or options remain available? |
| Decisions and evidence | Which choices require the author, which files implement the quest and which outcomes have current proof? |

## Access review

An optional beat may enrich understanding without becoming the sole gate to required progress. Compare the route with the beat and the route that skips the beat. A shared report may preserve distinct costs, allies or knowledge even when the report is the next scene for every route.

For a short new quest, an author may want a ferryman to reveal a river toll. The stable entry is the dock, the available action is to cross or turn back, and the route can end at the far bank or the dock. If crossing is mandatory, give the player another source of the toll beyond a declared optional dialogue or ask the author to change the dialogue requirement. Keep the conflicting gate visible until the choice is made.
